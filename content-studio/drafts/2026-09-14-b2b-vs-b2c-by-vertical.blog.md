# In Education, Winning a B2B Customer Costs 7.3× More Than Winning a B2C One

*By Mitchell Miller · ~5 min read · growth benchmarks / applied data*

## TL;DR

- **Acquisition cost, same vertical, both editions of the same report:** the B2B/B2C gap runs from **1.3×** in ecommerce ($86 vs $66) to **7.3×** in education ($1,143 vs $156). Across every vertical this dataset can match, not one number describes it.
- **"Conversion rate" is at least three numbers wearing one label.** The SaaS/software category converts at 3.8% on a landing page (Unbounce) and 7.6% site-wide (Ruler Analytics) — both true, neither publisher splits the figure by B2B vs. B2C, and they're not the same measurement anyway.
- **Reviews aren't the B2C-only habit they're assumed to be.** 86% of B2B software buyers turned to peer-review sites to reach a final decision, per G2's 2021 survey; roughly 93% of consumers do the same before an ordinary purchase. The gap most people assume is there mostly isn't.

## Acquisition cost: the range hiding inside the average

The most-repeated version of this stat compares two B2B figures from different industries: a B2B education customer costs **$1,143** to acquire; a B2B wholesale/ecommerce customer costs **$86** — same buyer type, same job, "get a business to buy," a 13× spread between the two priciest and cheapest verticals in First Page Sage's entire B2B report. That's real, and it's the widest gap in the B2B column. What it is *not* is a B2B-vs-B2C comparison, whatever the shorthand version implies — both numbers are First Page Sage's B2B figures. The actual B2C ecommerce number is separate and lower still: $66.

Do the same-vertical version instead — First Page Sage's B2B and B2C reports, matched industry to industry — and the picture changes. Ecommerce: $86 vs $66, **1.3×**. Real estate: $791 vs $165, **4.8×**. Education: $1,143 vs $156, **7.3×**. Healthcare has no B2B figure in either report at all — a real gap, not a rounding error. Pick your own vertical in the artifact above; the number that matters is the one for your market, not the dataset's extreme.

## Conversion rate isn't one number

This is the trap that catches the most benchmark decks. "SaaS converts at 3.8%" (Unbounce's SaaS landing-page category, 2024 data) and "software converts at 7.6%" (Ruler Analytics' Software category, full site, 2026) are not in disagreement — they're answering different questions, and neither publisher splits its figure by B2B vs. B2C in the first place. One measures a single landing page; the other measures every visit to the whole site. Layer in paid-search conversion (a third, separate figure) and you get three legitimate "conversion rate" numbers per vertical, and a lot of decks that quietly average two of them into a number no single report actually measured.

## Paid search: the same click costs 4× more in one vertical than another

WordStream's 2026 Google Ads benchmarks (13,000+ campaigns) put legal services at **$131.63** cost per lead against travel's **$44.70** — a gap driven almost entirely by cost-per-click ($9.87 vs $2.14), not by how often the ad gets clicked or the click converts. Three verticals in this dataset — SaaS, manufacturing, nonprofit — have no dedicated paid-search category at all in WordStream's 23-industry list. The artifact shows that as "no source," not a zero.

## The sales cycle that goes unmeasured on the B2C side

B2B sales-cycle length gets tracked in days by name — 60 for SaaS, 195 for financial services (sources disagree here by more than 2×; both are secondary and directional only), 162 for nonprofit B2B sales. B2C doesn't get the same treatment: no benchmark report in this pass quantifies "B2C sales cycle" in days the way B2B reports do, because consumer purchases are mostly treated as near-instant. That absence is itself the finding, not a missed search.

Touchpoints tell a similar story at a different scale: published B2B figures range from **27 to 417** depending on deal size and how "touchpoint" is defined, against roughly **6** for a consumer purchase. No single number is "the" B2B touchpoint count — the spread is the fact.

## How to read these numbers

- **Three funnel stages, never one axis.** Landing-page conversion (a single page), full-site conversion (any visit, anywhere), and paid-search conversion are different measurements. This piece and the artifact keep them in separate rows on purpose.
- **Report years and sample sizes, because they vary a lot.** Ruler Analytics: 110M+ sessions, 13 industries, 2026. Unbounce: 41,000 landing pages / 464M visitors, data through mid-2024. WordStream/LocaliQ: 13,000+ US Search campaigns, Apr 2025–Mar 2026. First Page Sage: anonymized client-analytics accounts, B2B edition data through Aug 2025 (published Jan 2026), B2C edition from 103 clients (published Jul 2025). Mailchimp's benchmark page was fetched September 2026 but states its underlying data was last updated December 2023 — still their current published figure, cited as such.
- **"Overall" means the source didn't split B2B from B2C.** Most conversion and paid-search figures here are industry-wide, not buyer-type-specific — marked plainly, never invented.

## FAQ

**Is B2B customer acquisition cost really higher than B2C?**
Usually, but the size of the gap depends entirely on the vertical — from roughly 1.3× in ecommerce to more than 7× in education, in matched First Page Sage data. A single blended "B2B costs more" number erases that range.

**Why do B2B and B2C conversion rate benchmarks disagree so much?**
Mostly because "conversion rate" describes at least three different funnel stages — a landing page, a full site, or a paid-search click — that different publishers measure differently. Two accurate numbers for the same vertical can look contradictory if you don't check which stage each one is measuring.

**Do B2B buyers read reviews as much as B2C shoppers?**
In the one vertical with sourced data on both sides, yes, almost as much: 86% of B2B software buyers turned to peer-review sites before a final decision (G2's 2021 B2B Software Behavior Survey) versus roughly 93% of consumers generally. The "reviews are a B2C thing" assumption doesn't hold up where it can actually be checked.
