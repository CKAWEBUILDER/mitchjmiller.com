# Ranking a New Site in a Market Legacy Competitors Own

*By Mitchell Miller · ~11 min read · Local search / AI visibility*

## The short answer

To rank a new website in a competitive market, stop chasing the head term. Go after coverage: the channels incumbents have left empty, OTAs included, and the local questions they have not answered. Treat long-tail pages as a way in, not a better closer, and don't expect FAQ markup to win AI citations: Google says no special schema is needed.

## TL;DR

- **AI Overviews are scarce on local intent.** seoClarity, across 500M+ U.S. keywords: 0.14% of local keywords in March 2025, "just 0.01%" by September 2025. Whitespark's hand-collected 540-query sample (2025): 15% of local-intent searches, but 92% of informational local-business searches.
- **Google says schema is not required.** Its guide to generative AI features: "Structured data isn't required for generative AI search" (2026). FAQ rich results stopped appearing in Search on May 7, 2026.
- **In the one peer-reviewed test, longer keywords converted worse.** Ghose and Yang (NYU Stern, 2008), organic search at one U.S. retailer: "an increase in length of the keyword by 1 word decreases conversion rate by 5.41%."
- **Distribution is the bigger gap.** Arival's operator survey: OTAs "surging to 37% of bookings in 2025." About 22.9 million experiences were booked through Viator alone, on $4.7B gross booking value (Tripadvisor 10-K, FY2025).
- **Research volume is the real cost.** On a 90-day Waikiki pilot I crawled and read 973 competitor pages across ten businesses. Search Console clicks went from 5 to 54 and impressions from 470 to 1,652 across two consecutive 28-day windows (2026).

## Why can't a new site just target the head term?

Because the head term is years of accumulated evidence, and you have none of it. In a destination market the incumbents hold the domain age, the review volume, the map pack and the OTA listings. In Waikiki, three incumbent surf schools each show or claim more than a thousand reviews. "Surf lessons waikiki" is not a ranking problem you solve in a quarter.

That part of the standard advice is right. The next two steps are where it goes wrong.

## Does long-tail content convert better?

Not on the evidence I could find. It is cheaper to reach, which is a different argument.

The one peer-reviewed measurement I found runs against the folklore. Ghose and Yang analysed 13 weeks of paid and organic search data from one U.S. nationwide retailer (776 keywords, early 2007). In organic search, "an increase in length of the keyword by 1 word decreases conversion rate by 5.41%." Their companion paper measured sponsored-search click-through falling 6.6% per additional word.

This matters because the usual pitch for long-tail is "lower volume, higher intent, better conversion." The first holds by definition. The third is measured going the other way.

Keep the tactic, change the reason. Long-tail is where a new domain can actually place, and where competition is thin enough that one good page ranks. That is an access argument, and it is enough.

**Label:** sourced (Ghose and Yang, 2008). One retailer, 776 keywords, 13 weeks of 2007 data. Whether that transfers to a 2026 local service is genuinely open; I could not find a modern replication. Treat it as one dated measurement that cuts against the folklore, not as a law.

## Will FAQ schema get me cited by AI?

Not by Google's own account, and the one controlled test I found agrees.

Google's guide to its generative AI features lists "overfocusing on structured data" among the things you can ignore: "Structured data isn't required for generative AI search, and there's no special schema.org markup you need to add." FAQ markup then lost its visible payoff in Search as well. HowTo rich results were deprecated in 2023, and Google's update log records of FAQ rich results: "This feature will no longer appear in Google Search starting May 7, 2026."

The only matched difference-in-differences test I found, from Ahrefs, followed 1,885 pages that added JSON-LD, each against three control pages. AI Overview citations fell 4.6% relative to the controls; AI Mode (+2.4%) and ChatGPT (+2.2%) moved within noise. Every page in it was already heavily cited, so it says nothing direct about a site with no citations yet.

Two things circulating that will not survive a reader who checks:

- A statistic credited to Princeton CS and Moz, said to cover 500,000 URLs and to have appeared at WWW 2026, presented as proof that FAQ schema multiplies AI citations. I could not find the paper at WWW 2026, on arXiv, in the ACM Digital Library or at Princeton CS. The only source I could trace is an unsigned page with no DOI that closes by advertising its own schema generator.
- The arXiv work on "structured" content (GEO-SFE, CITECHOICE) tests headings, short paragraphs, lists and tables, not JSON-LD. CITECHOICE's own arms are "polished prose" versus "polished structured text." That is evidence for writing clearly, not for markup.

Mark up your pages. Schema keeps you eligible for the rich results Google still shows, supports entity understanding, and costs little. Just do not budget a quarter against it expecting AI citations.

**Label:** sourced (Google Search Central; Ahrefs). Contradicted, specifically, is the AI-citation claim, not the general value of structured data.

## Where should the effort go instead?

Coverage. Two kinds.

**Surface coverage.** In tours and activities the booking does not necessarily start or end on your site. Arival's operator survey reports OTAs "surging to 37% of bookings in 2025." Viator alone recorded approximately 22.9 million experiences booked, on $4.7 billion in gross booking value, in FY2025, per Tripadvisor's 10-K. A new operator absent from the channel carrying more than a third of operators' bookings has a distribution gap, not a ranking gap.

So the audit question is not "what do we rank for." It is: which surfaces do the incumbents occupy, and which have they left empty? Search, maps, the OTAs, video, review platforms, community threads. Map all of it, then go where they are not.

**Question coverage.** The queries a prospective customer types in the destination that the incumbents have not answered. Best time of day for a beginner. What to bring. Which break suits which ability. These are not high volume. They are winnable, and they are the questions a real operator can answer better than a marketplace listing.

They are also where AI answers do appear. Whitespark found AI Overviews on 92% of informational local-business searches, against 15% of local-intent ones. So write each answer plainly enough to be quoted; Google says no special markup is needed to appear in its AI features. The split by intent is charted in [Search results by intent](https://mj2.pro/blog/search-results-by-intent/).

| Move | Effort | Time to signal | Owner | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Map incumbent surface coverage | High (one-time) | Immediate | Strategy | Finds the empty channel, not another keyword |
| List on the OTAs carrying category share | Medium | Weeks | Operations | Arival: 37% of bookings, 2025 |
| Answer unanswered local questions | Medium, ongoing | 1–3 months | Content | Thin competition; a new domain can place |
| Paid search on brand and head terms | Low | Days | Paid | Fastest test of whether demand converts |
| Schema markup | Low | n/a | Engineering | Rich results and entity clarity — not AI citation |

## What does the exhaustive analysis actually look like?

Export everything, then query it like a database. Competitor page inventories, ranking keyword sets, channel presence, review counts, listing completeness. Volume is the point: you are looking for absence, and absence only shows up against a full map.

On the Waikiki pilot that meant crawling and reading 973 pages across ten competing businesses (the [search visibility report](https://mj2.pro/case-studies/sfc-surf-school/) shows the method). Then the useful question becomes answerable: which of these operators is missing from which surface, and what has none of them written about?

Language models are good at the organising step — normalising exports, clustering pages by intent, running the set-difference queries that find gaps. They are not good at deciding which gap is worth a quarter. That stays with you.

## Limits

- **Arival is a single research house** on the OTA numbers, and Phocuswright is its co-author rather than an independent check. Read it as "Arival's operator survey finds," not "the market is." The sub-splits circulating for direct-booking share are not on Arival's own page and I could not verify them.
- **No touchpoint count exists for tours and activities.** Expedia's widely cited "141 pages of travel content in the 45 days prior to booking" measures pages, not touchpoints, for trips booked across travel broadly. A surf lesson is not a week-long trip. Google's own messy-middle research contains no touchpoint count at all. I left the number out rather than stretch it.
- **The local AI Overview figures disagree.** seoClarity's 0.01% and Whitespark's 15% measure different query sets — Whitespark's sample is 540 queries across three cities, manually collected, and its circulated "68%" is a mixed-intent set. Both point the same direction; neither is precise.
- **The schema test used pages that were already cited.** Every page in Ahrefs' test had 100+ AI Overview citations before it added schema, so the result says nothing direct about a new site. Google's documentation carries that part of the argument.
- **The SFC numbers are small in absolute terms.** Five clicks to 54 is a real change on a tiny base, and it is a single site over one 28-day comparison. It is directional evidence, not proof of method.

## What I actually did

SFC is an independent surf school on Waikiki's South Shore. Over a 90-day pilot in 2026 I ran the competitive research across 973 pages, rebuilt the lesson and guide architecture, and built a geographically grounded ten-break explorer with original-source video links and ten connected break guides, alongside Google Business Profile campaign planning and controlled staging-to-production releases.

Measured in Search Console, comparing August 12 – September 8 against July 15 – August 11, 2026: clicks moved from 5 to 54, impressions from 470 to 1,652 ([the readout](https://mj2.pro/lab/)).

The honest caveat, which matters more than the headline numbers: the ten-break explorer launched September 10, *after* that measurement window closed. Whatever moved those numbers, it was not the piece of the build I am proudest of. That is the kind of thing a 90-day readout tells you only if you date your windows.

The pattern underneath is the same one I used at scale in healthcare: size the opportunity against what the established players have already covered, then publish where they have not. There it was a conditions-and-treatments library, planned by crawling competing medical libraries against a 10,000-term universe. Here it is surf-break and planning guides, aimed at searches where travel publishers win and no surf school competes. Same shape, very different scale.

## FAQ

**Should a new local site skip schema entirely?**
No. Schema still supports legitimate rich results and helps engines resolve your entity. The claim worth dropping is that adding FAQ markup buys AI citations: Google's documentation says no special markup is required for its AI features, and FAQ rich results stopped appearing in Search in May 2026.

**If AI Overviews barely appear on local queries, is AI visibility irrelevant for local businesses?**
No. They are rare on local-intent searches but common on the informational questions around them: Whitespark found AI Overviews on 92% of informational local-business searches. Google also says its AI features can show what is being said about products and services across the web, including blogs, videos and forum discussions. That argues for plain answers on your site and presence on those surfaces, not markup.

**How long before a new site sees movement?**
On the pilot above, clicks went from 5 to 54 between two consecutive 28-day windows. Small base, single site. Plan in quarters, and date your measurement windows so you can tell which change did the work.

**Is paid search worth it for a new operator?**
It is the fastest way to learn whether the demand converts at all, before you spend a quarter on content. If incumbents are not bidding, it is also unusually cheap — check before assuming.

**What is the single highest-leverage first step?**
Map where the incumbents already appear and where they do not. Absence only shows up against a complete map, which is why the research volume is the cost.

### 5. Decisions for Mitch

1. **The thesis reverses three of your own premises.** Local intent has almost no AI Overviews; long-tail converted worse in the one peer-reviewed test; FAQ schema is not the AI lever; distribution through OTAs is. The first holds only for local-intent searches: the informational questions you planned to target are where Whitespark saw AI Overviews on 92% of searches. The post now says so.
2. **Schema tension with a live post.** `/blog/optimizing-for-ads-in-free-llm-answers/` calls the schema question "unresolved"; this post leans on Google's documentation and the Ahrefs test. Both can stand, but decide whether to align the wording or cross-link.
3. **The Princeton/Moz bullet.** It now names the circulating claim without its multiplier and says only that the paper could not be found. Cut the bullet entirely if you would rather not repeat the attribution at all.
